Challenge a method

Under a condition you identify yourself, does a sampling method become more or less reliable, and does the evidence agree with your hypothesis?

Learning objective
Form a testable hypothesis about how a selection rule interacts with a population's values or order, test it fairly with repeated samples, and interpret the result, whether or not it supports you.
Reader
The health professional, who should be able to change your condition themselves and see how the methods respond.
Data
The baseline population, data/{{sc file}}, and a labelled modified copy {{help modified-population}} that your users control. The same total n in every comparison.

Finished result

Users can apply your condition to a labelled modified copy and compare, at the same total n, your chosen method under baseline and modified conditions and one alternative method or justified correction under the corresponding baseline and modified conditions. Each result's bias is measured against its own population's true mean.

Decide

First, alone: write your hypothesis Required first

Do this before any conversation with your agent about this stage. Write it in DECISIONS.md, section 5, with today's date, and do not edit it afterwards. This guide does not answer these questions for you:

  • Which method will you challenge: simple random, systematic or stratified?
  • What change to your population's values, membership or row order will you test?
  • Why would that method's selection rule interact with the change? What does the rule “see”, and what does it miss?
  • What do you expect across repeated samples: how spread out the estimates will be, and where their centre will fall relative to the true mean?
  • Which alternative method or correction will you compare it with, and why?
  • What result would contradict your hypothesis?

Keep in mind: the specified sampling designs, with their correct estimators, are unbiased for the finite-population mean. A method can stay unbiased and still become less reliable, because its estimates vary more from sample to sample. You do not need to produce bias or “break” a method. A hypothesis that is not supported, or a method that stays reliable, is a valid result to explain.

More help

Bias, variability and simulation noise

Bias is the average of a method's estimates minus the true mean of the population being sampled. For a modified population that is its recalculated true mean, not the baseline's. The specified designs and estimators are unbiased for that mean, so across all possible samples the average estimate matches it. What can differ between conditions is how much the estimates vary around it.

With random repetitions you only see a finite number of samples, so the bias you measure is usually not exactly zero even for an unbiased method. That nonzero value may be simulation noise; it is not guaranteed to be small, and its sign can change with the seed. Compare several seeds before reading anything into it. Exact enumeration of systematic sampling uses every possible start, so it involves no simulation noise and no seed.

RMSE combines both: RMSE² = bias² + SD². If RMSE rises while bias stays near zero, the SD is what rose.

What counts as an alternative or a correction?

An alternative is a different one of the three methods, run under the same baseline and modified conditions. A correction is a change you justify that keeps the sampling rules correct, run on both the baseline and the modified population so it is compared fairly. Either way, use the same total n as the method you challenged.

Why can't my agent help me come up with the hypothesis?

Devising the hypothesis is the critical reasoning this stage assesses. Your agent can explain terms, ask clarifying questions and help you build and verify the test once your idea is written. The interpretation is yours.

Does reordering rows change the true mean?

No: the same rows in a different order have the same mean. Changing values, or adding or removing rows, may change it, so recalculate it every time. Changing N can also change whether a systematic interval is a whole number, so check which design applies.

My hypothesis wasn't supported. What now?

That is a valid result. Check the test was fair (same n, enough repetitions, correct rules and weights, true mean recalculated, results labelled and not pooled). Then explain what the evidence shows and what else might explain it. If you revise your hypothesis, add a dated revision below the original rather than replacing it.

Claims to avoid
  • “This method is biased” based on one unlucky draw, or on a nonzero bias from random repetitions that changes with the seed.
  • “Simple random sampling is biased.” It is unbiased for the mean; one bad draw is variability.
  • “This method always (or never) works.” Any result depends on the population, row order, n and condition you tested.
  • Calling wrong stratum weights a failure of stratification. That is a calculation error.
  • Changing a sampling rule, or comparing conditions at different n, to force a result.
  • Measuring a modified result's bias against the baseline's true mean.

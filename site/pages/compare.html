Compare methods & sample size

Across many samples, which method's estimates stay close to the true mean, and how does sample size change how reliable they are?

Learning objective
Use repeated samples {{help sampling-distribution}} to tell bias {{help bias}} (consistent error) from variability {{help variability}} (random scatter), summarise both with RMSE {{help rmse}}, and show how the spread of estimates changes with n {{help sample-size}}.
Reader
The health professional deciding which method to trust, and how large a sample to pay for.
Data
The baseline population {{help baseline}}: data/{{sc file}} as supplied. The population stays the same in both experiments.

Finished result

Your app runs two experiments on the baseline population and shows each current result's distribution of estimates against the labelled true mean, with bias, SD of estimates and RMSE, labelled Monte Carlo or exact with its settings.

Three measures you will read

  • Bias {{help bias}}: Is the average estimate off target? The mean of the estimates minus the true mean.
  • SD of estimates {{help variability}}: How much do estimates vary between samples? This is the spread of the repeated estimates, not the spread of individual {{sc target}} values in the population.
  • RMSE {{help rmse}}: How large are estimation errors overall? It combines bias and SD.

All three are in {{sc units}}. Formulas are behind the ? buttons and in the glossary.

The two experiments are fixed; how you present them is your design.

  1. Experiment A: compare the three methods at n = 100. Simple random {{help srs}}, systematic {{help systematic}} and stratified {{help stratified}} sampling, same population, same n.
  2. Experiment B: investigate sample size. Simple random sampling only, at n = 1, 10 and 100, with the population and method held fixed so n is the only thing that changes. Reuse the simple random n = 100 results from experiment A when the population and settings match; otherwise rerun them.

For each condition, use at least 100 random repetitions (default 100, with a control to increase to 1,000), or all k random starts for systematic sampling with a whole-number interval (exact enumeration). {{help repeated-sampling}} {{help exact-enumeration}}

Sample size and repetitions are separate settings

n is the number of observations in one sample; the repetition count is how many separate samples your app draws. At n = 10 with 100 repetitions, the app draws 100 separate samples of 10 observations and calculates 100 sample means. Each sample is drawn without replacement, so no observation appears twice within a sample; an observation may appear again in a later repetition.

100 repetitions usually reveal the main pattern; 1,000 produce smoother distributions and more stable bias, SD and RMSE estimates. Neither count guarantees stable results, and 100 repetitions cannot reliably distinguish small differences between methods. Rerun with different seeds to check whether your main conclusions hold.

More help

When do I use exact enumeration?

For this activity, use exact enumeration for whole-number systematic sampling and Monte Carlo for the other required results. With a whole-number interval k = N/n, the random start is the only random step, so there are just k equally likely samples; listing all k gives the exact distribution, and drawing random starts would only repeat them. Simple random sampling, stratified sampling and the fractional-interval design use seeded random repetitions, labelled Monte Carlo. For your file, the interval at each size is:

{{intervals clinic}} {{intervals costs}} {{intervals heart}}

Exact results do not depend on a seed. Monte Carlo results do, so compare several seeds before trusting small differences.

What does RMSE add to bias and SD?

RMSE is the typical size of the error of one estimate, counting both a consistent offset (bias) and random scatter (SD): RMSE² = bias² + SD², with SD calculated by dividing by the number of repetitions (or starts). It is in the same units as your target, so you can say “a typical estimate is off by about … {{sc units}}”.

Unbiased does not mean reliable

The specified designs and estimators are unbiased for the finite-population mean: across all possible samples the average estimate equals the true mean. A method can stay unbiased and still be less reliable, because its estimates vary more from sample to sample. A larger sample narrows that spread; it cannot repair an estimate that is consistently off for some other reason, such as a wrong weight or a changed population.

With random repetitions you only see a finite number of samples, so the bias you measure is usually not exactly zero even for an unbiased method. That nonzero value may be simulation noise; it is not guaranteed to be small, and its sign can change with the seed. Compare several seeds, or increase to 1,000 repetitions, before reading anything into it.

Why keep the population and method fixed in experiment B?

To isolate one cause. If anything else changes along with n, you cannot tell which change produced the difference.

Optional extension: other methods across sample sizes

Compare systematic or stratified sampling across sizes one method at a time, with the same repetitions per size. Every size must follow that method's rules. Systematic: use the whole-number interval when N/n is whole and the fractional interval otherwise; never drop a size because N/n is not whole. Stratified: n must be at least the number of strata and no larger than N.

Troubleshooting
  • Numbers change every time I run it. Check the seed is shown, fixed and passed to the shared sampling module. The same seed and settings must give the same numbers.
  • The simple random n = 100 numbers differ between experiments. The seed, repetition count or population differs. Rerun both with identical settings.
  • It runs slowly. Ask your agent to run the repetitions once, in the shared module, and reuse the numbers for every display. Do not lower the repetition count below 100.
  • A result is missing a label. Ask your agent to show population, method, settings, seed, n and repetition count beside every result.
Claims to avoid
  • Calling a method biased because one sample missed the true mean, or because a nonzero Monte Carlo bias changes sign with the seed.
  • “Smaller spread means no bias.” Spread and bias are separate questions.
  • “A bigger sample is always closer to the truth.” Larger samples are closer on average; any one sample can still miss.
  • Calling a method the best without saying for which population, which n, and by which measure.
  • Combining estimates from different seeds, methods, sizes or conditions into one set, or changing the method or population along with n.
